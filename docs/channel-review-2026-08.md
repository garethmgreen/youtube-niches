## The call

**Restart it. Don't resume it.**

The demand is far bigger than it was when the channel went dark, and the available tools
are newer than its best video. But the format — four-minute AI clips plus a wall of Shorts
— is the worst-performing shape in this niche now, and it was already failing while uploads
were still going out. Posting more of the same would replay the decline below.

Keep the channel, the name and the brand. Change the length, pick a lane, and fix the
authenticity framing before a single new upload goes live.

## Part one — What the channel's own numbers say

### The collapse happened while uploads were still going out

The assumed story is that the channel took off and then faded through lost interest. The
upload record says otherwise: videos 1–3 caught the standard new-channel boost, and videos
4–9 fell off a cliff **before** uploads stopped. The quitting followed the decline; it
didn't cause it.

That reframes the problem. It was never a consistency problem — it was a content-shape
problem, and consistency alone would not have fixed it.

### Three findings

- **10:24 — the only video over ten minutes.** It is also the only real hit, at 21,541
  views, more than the other eight combined. Everything winning in this niche today is long.
- **0.34% — like rate on that hit.** 74 likes on 21,541 views, against a healthy 2–4%.
  Even the winner wasn't holding people: the views arrived, the audience didn't.
- **0.15% — views converted to subscribers.** 80,809 lifetime views produced 124 subs.
  That is the signature of Shorts-feed traffic — it inflates the view count and builds
  nothing you keep.

### What the 33 Shorts bought

Roughly 43,700 views between them — over half the lifetime total — best one at 6.5K, most
around 1,000. In exchange for a large share of production effort they delivered a feed
audience that does not follow through to a long video. Shorts should be a trailer for a
film that exists, not the product.

## Part three — What actually won

Three distinct products, not variations on a theme. Different audiences, different lengths,
moving in different directions right now.

### Cluster A — AI feature films (45–90 min, fiction) · ascending

[AI Trailer Cinema](https://www.youtube.com/@AItrailercinema) is the category: 221K subs,

one from the next. Crowded with sub-1K-view channels, almost none monetized.

## Part four — The monetization gate

On **15 July 2026** YouTube renamed its "repetitious content" rule to **"inauthentic
content"** in the Partner Program. It targets mass-produced, repetitive uploads lacking
genuine originality — synthetic narration over interchangeable footage, every video looking
and sounding the same. Thousands of faceless AI channels lost monetization under it, and in
January 2026 YouTube terminated sixteen channels holding a combined 35M subscribers.

Of the **fifteen AI-built channels surveyed, ten are not monetized.** This is the base rate
in this niche, not a hypothetical.

### What the five monetized ones do differently

They disclose, and they add real editorial work. Cave Stories states its AI use directly in
its channel About — *"How I use AI: The voiceover uses standard, generic…"* — and builds
each video on a genuine researched incident. AI as a production tool applied to real
material, not as the material itself.

### The specific exposure

The channel description says stories are *"rooted in real events but told through my own
characters"*; video copy says *"based on real events."* Invented stories presented in the
grammar of survivor testimony is precisely the pattern the policy is written to catch.

Fiction does not need to be abandoned — it needs to stop being dressed as documentary.

## Part five — The decision

### Lane A — AI survival features

Compete directly with AI Trailer Cinema. Openly fictional, feature-length, genre-cinema framing.

- **Ceiling:** highest in the niche; million-view videos landing this month
- **Cost:** heaviest — a 60-minute AI film is a real production
- **Policy risk:** low; clearly labelled fiction has no authenticity problem
- **Catch:** it is a genre-film business, not the original idea. Incumbent has a two-year
  head start and 226 films shipped.

### Lane B — Real incidents, reconstructed

Compete with Cave Stories and Extreme Survival Stories. Researched true events, AI-assisted
reconstruction, disclosed.

- **Ceiling:** proven but softening
- **Cost:** moderate production, plus real research hours that can't be faked
- **Policy risk:** low *if* disclosed and the research is genuine — this is where the
  monetized channels sit
- **Catch:** closest to the ISBA spirit, but entering a cooling format against established
  libraries

### Recommended — feature-length first-person testimony

The gap nobody in this data occupies. The AI-movie channels all make **third-person genre
films**. The documentary channels all make **third-person narration** at 10–25 minutes. Not
one is doing the thing that made *I Shouldn't Be Alive* work: **the survivor's own voice, in
the first person, at feature length.**

That was the original instinct. The channel name is already built on it, and the one genuine
hit — "48 DAYS LOST" — used it most directly. Everyone else dropped the half of the format
that carries the emotion, because third-person narration is easier to script. Doing the
harder half is what makes it difficult to clone when the next wave arrives.

It also resolves the policy problem cleanly: a first-person account of an *invented*
character is a radio drama, labelled as one. A survival story, told as fiction, at the
length of a film. No documentary claim, no ambiguity, no exposure.

## Part six — First five videos back

One environment each, no repeats, in this order. Every title carries a first-person voice, a
hard number, and one specific irreversible moment the thumbnail can show. Target 45–60
minutes. One a fortnight — five videos over ten weeks beats twenty over the same period.

**1. "I Was Adrift For 54 Days. On Day 12 I Made A Choice I Still Can't Explain."**
52 min · open ocean · proven environment
Open on water — the one setting the audience already showed up for (the 21K video and the
best Shorts are all lost-at-sea). Lead with strength while rebuilding signal.
*Thumbnail:* raft dead-centre and tiny, swallowed by a slate-green swell shot from high
above. One orange flare as the only warm thing in frame. Two words: DAY 12.

**2. "The Avalanche Buried Four Of Us. Only I Could Move My Hand."**
48 min · alpine · confinement
Claustrophobia is the strongest hook in the niche — Cave Stories built 38.5K subs almost
entirely on buried-alive scenarios. The single moving hand is a visual the whole film pays off.
*Thumbnail:* packed blue-white snow filling frame, one bare hand breaking the surface. No
face. Near-monochrome so the hand is the only event.

**3. "I Survived The Jungle By Following A Bird. It Took 19 Days."**
55 min · Amazon · curiosity hook
The odd specific detail makes it clickable — "following a bird" is a question the title
doesn't answer. The jungle clip did 6.4K on the weakest possible format, so appetite is proven.
*Thumbnail:* dense canopy from below, one shaft of light, a small pale bird sharp in focus
while the figure beneath is blurred and half-lost. Text: 19 DAYS.

**4. "Trapped 400 Feet Down For 9 Days: I Heard The Rescuers Give Up."**
50 min · cave/mine · peak tension
Strongest premise placed fourth, once watch history is re-established. "I heard them give
up" is the most emotionally loaded line in the slate.
*Thumbnail:* total black with a single headlamp cone on wet rock, figure small at the bottom
edge. The darkness is most of the image — it will out-contrast every bright thumbnail beside it.

**5. "Three Of Us Walked Into The Desert. I Am Telling You This Alone."**
47 min · desert · format test
New environment, and the one title stating its ending in its first line. Tests whether the
first-person promise alone carries a click without a number in the hook.
*Thumbnail:* bleached horizon, three sets of footprints where only one continues past the
midpoint. No figure at all — the absence is the image.

## Part seven — Fix before uploading anything

An afternoon's work, and every item currently works against the channel.

1. **Description** — cut "rooted in real events" and "based on real events" from the About
   and all nine video descriptions. Replace with a plain line: original fictional survival
   stories, produced with AI. Highest-value edit on the list.
2. **Disclosure** — add an explicit AI-use note to About, the way the monetized channels do.
   Tick the altered-content disclosure on every upload. This is what separates the five
   monetized AI channels from the ten that aren't.
3. **Shorts** — leave the 33 up, they do no harm, but stop making them as primary output.
   Future Shorts get cut *from* a finished long video, pointing at it.
4. **Titles** — retitle the six failed videos into first person. They won't suddenly
   perform, but the channel will read as one coherent product.
5. **Captions** — the hit video has none. Add them everywhere; this niche has heavy
   silent-autoplay traffic and it costs nothing.
6. **Banner** — currently sells an anthology of clips. It should sell one thing:
   feature-length survival stories, told by the person who lived them.

## Method & caveats

Channel and competitor figures pulled from the Nexlev YouTube dataset and live YouTube data
on 26 August 2026.

- Subscriber and view counts above ~1,000 are **rounded by the source APIs**.
- Competitor publication dates come back **bucketed by month** — month-level timing is
  approximate, day-level precision is unavailable.
- Revenue figures are **AdSense estimates only**; they exclude sponsorship, affiliate and
  product income. Read as order-of-magnitude.
- "Monetized" reflects the dataset's **detection flag**, not confirmed Partner Program
  status. The 10-of-15 ratio is directionally solid but shouldn't be quoted as exact.
- The nine long-form view counts and the 21,541 views / 74 likes figure are **exact reads**
  from the video records.
